When your wood fence went in, it was a warm honey color. A few years later it's
dull gray, maybe with black or green patches. The good news: most of the time
that color isn't gone — it's hiding under a thin damaged layer, and the right
cleaning brings it back. Here's what happened to your fence and how to fix it
without wrecking the wood.

## The sun did most of it

Wood turns gray for the same reason your skin burns: UV rays. Sunlight breaks
down lignin, the natural glue that holds wood fibers together and gives wood
its color. As the lignin on the surface breaks apart, the wood fades to gray.
The key word is *surface* — UV graying is usually only a paper-thin layer deep.
The fresh-looking wood is still right underneath.

## The Gulf Coast added the rest

Around Mobile Bay, the sun has a partner: moisture. Our humidity and rain keep
fences damp for long stretches, and damp wood grows mildew and algae. That's
where the black patches, green film, and blotchy dark streaks come from.
Mildew isn't just ugly — it holds even more moisture against the wood, which
speeds up rot at the posts and rails. A gray fence here is almost always sun
damage and growth working together.

## The high-pressure mistake

Here's where a lot of DIY fence cleaning goes wrong. Wood is soft. Hit it with
a pressure washer turned up high, held too close, and you don't clean the fence
— you carve it. The pressure tears out the softer wood between the grain lines
and leaves the surface rough and hairy, a condition called **furring**. Once a
fence is furred, it splinters, soaks up water faster, and grays again quicker
than before. You'll also see wand marks — light and dark stripes that follow
every pass. That damage doesn't buff out.

## How wood should be cleaned

The right approach is gentle: a wood-safe cleaning solution that kills the
mildew and loosens the gray, weathered fibers, followed by a low-pressure
rinse. The cleaner does the work, not the pressure. Done right, the rinse
sweeps away the thin gray layer and the growth, and the wood's real color comes
back — often dramatically. Old, deeply weathered boards may not return all the
way to day-one color, but the difference is usually night and day.

This goes for your **deck boards** too, by the way. Decks take even more direct
sun than fences, plus foot traffic, so they gray faster — and they're just as
easy to ruin with high pressure. Same gentle method applies.

## Seal it while it's clean

A freshly cleaned fence is bare, open wood — which means it will start graying
again as soon as the sun gets back to work. The smart move is to let it dry for
a few days, then apply a **stain or sealer with UV protection**. That layer
takes the sun's abuse so the wood doesn't have to, and it sheds water so mildew
has less to feed on. Clean first, always: sealing over gray fibers and mildew
just locks the problem in.

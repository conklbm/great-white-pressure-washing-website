Those orange streaks on your driveway aren't dirt. They're rust — iron that has
soaked into the surface of the concrete and stained it, kind of like tea stains
a white mug. That's why scrubbing and hosing don't touch them. Here's what does
work, and when it's smarter to hand the job off.

## Where the rust is coming from

Around Mobile and Baldwin County, rust stains almost always trace back to one of
a few sources:

- **Sprinkler water.** A lot of wells and irrigation systems here pull water
  with iron in it. Every time the sprinklers hit the driveway, they leave a
  little more orange behind.
- **Fertilizer.** Many lawn fertilizers contain iron. Spill some granules on wet
  concrete and each one leaves a tiny rust dot.
- **Metal furniture and equipment.** Patio chairs, grills, plant stands, and
  basketball goals rust at the feet and bleed onto whatever they sit on.
- **Battery acid — usually not.** People often blame a parked car's battery for
  driveway stains. True battery acid damage is rare and looks like etched,
  eaten-away concrete, not an orange stain. If the mark is orange, it's almost
  certainly plain rust.

## Why household cleaners fail

Bleach is the first thing most people reach for, and it does nothing here.
Bleach kills living stuff — algae, mildew, mold. Rust isn't alive. It's a
mineral stain sitting down inside the pores of the concrete, so bleach, dish
soap, and all-purpose sprays just rinse over the top of it. A pressure washer
alone won't do it either. Pressure removes what's *on* the concrete, not what
has soaked *into* it.

## What actually works: oxalic acid

Rust needs a cleaner made for rust. The workhorse is **oxalic acid**, the
active ingredient in most concrete rust removers at the hardware store. It
reacts with the iron and dissolves the stain instead of trying to scrub it off.

The basic DIY steps:

1. Wet the concrete first.
2. Apply the rust remover and let it sit as long as the label says.
3. Scrub with a stiff brush, then rinse well.
4. Repeat — older stains usually take more than one round.

A word of caution: these are real acids. Wear gloves and eye protection, keep
the product off your grass and plants, and never mix it with bleach or any
other cleaner.

A few fresh rust dots from a flower pot? DIY is a fine choice. A weekend and a
bottle of rust remover will probably handle it.

## An honest note

Some rust stains won't come out 100 percent. If iron-heavy water has been
hitting the same spot for ten years, the stain can go deep enough that even a
strong treatment lightens it a lot but doesn't erase it completely. Anyone who
promises perfection on old, deep rust is guessing. What you can count on is a
big improvement — most stains lift fully, and the stubborn ones fade to where
you have to look for them.

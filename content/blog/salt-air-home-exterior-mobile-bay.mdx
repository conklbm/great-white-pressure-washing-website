Ever licked your lips after an afternoon on the water and tasted salt? If you
live near the bay, your house tastes it too. Mobile Bay is saltwater fed from
the Gulf, and the breeze off it carries a fine mist that lands on everything —
siding, windows, railings, light fixtures. It's gentler than what homes right
on the open Gulf deal with, but on a bayfront street it's an everyday fact.
Here's what it's doing to your place and how to stay ahead of it.

## Salt plus humidity is the problem

Salt on its own is just a dusty film. Mix it with our humidity and it turns
into something worse. Salt pulls moisture out of the air and holds it against
your home's surfaces, so painted wood, metal, and caulk lines stay damp far
longer than they would a few miles inland. Damp surfaces break down faster.
Paint fades and chalks, caulk cracks, and that constant dampness feeds the
mildew and algae that turn siding dingy and green.

Distance from the water matters a lot. A home on Scenic 98 in Point Clear or a
bluff-top house in Fairhope catches noticeably more than one a mile back from
the shoreline, and both catch more than a place out toward Semmes.

## Metal shows it first

Look at the hinges, door handles, light fixtures, and railings on any
waterfront home that hasn't been washed in a while. You'll see rust — and under
it, orange streaks bleeding down the wall or the concrete below. Salt speeds up
corrosion, which is why homes near the water chew through fixtures, fasteners,
and outdoor hardware faster. Rinsing the film off regularly won't make metal
immortal, but it slows the process down and stops rust streaks from staining
everything underneath.

## Docks, decks, and the things that live outside

Bayfront living means more stuff exposed to the weather: boat house railings,
pier decking, outdoor kitchens, screened porches facing the water. These take
the salt, the sun, and the damp all at once, and they're usually the first
things a guest notices. Wood especially — salt film keeps the surface damp,
and damp wood grays and grows mildew faster than dry wood ever will.

## Why waterfront homes need washing more often

A home out in West Mobile might do fine with a wash once a year. Bayfront homes
often can't stretch quite that far, because the salt keeps landing and the damp
film it leaves keeps feeding growth. Somewhere between six months and a year is
the usual rhythm near the water, depending on how close you are and which
direction your walls face. North-facing walls in shade are always the first to
go green.

## Wash it gently

The right way to clean a salt-coated home is
[soft washing](/services/house-soft-washing) — low pressure and a cleaning
solution that dissolves the film and kills the mildew, followed by a thorough
rinse. High pressure is the wrong tool here. Waterfront homes take enough of a
beating from the weather; blasting sun-weakened paint and caulk with a pressure
wand just speeds up the damage the salt started. Same goes for
[wood decks and railings](/services/deck-fence-cleaning), which fur up badly
under too much pressure.

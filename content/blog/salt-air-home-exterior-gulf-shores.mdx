Ever licked your lips after a day at the beach and tasted salt? Your house
tastes it every single day. In Gulf Shores and Orange Beach, the air itself
carries a fine mist of salt water off the Gulf, and it lands on everything —
siding, windows, railings, light fixtures. Over time, that salt does real
damage. Here's what it's doing to your place and how to stay ahead of it.

## Salt plus humidity is the problem

Salt on its own is just a dusty film. But mix it with our humidity and it turns
into something worse. Salt pulls moisture out of the air and holds it against
your home's surfaces, so painted wood, metal, and caulk lines stay damp far
longer than they would inland. Damp surfaces break down faster. Paint fades and
chalks, caulk cracks, and that ever-present dampness feeds the mildew and algae
that turn siding dingy and green.

The closer you are to the water, the heavier the salt load. A house on the
beach road catches more spray in a month than a Foley home sees in a year.

## Metal shows it first

Look at the hinges, door handles, light fixtures, and railings on any beach
home that hasn't been washed in a while. You'll see rust — and under it, orange
streaks bleeding down the wall or the concrete below. Salt is brutal on metal.
It speeds up corrosion, which is why beach houses chew through fixtures,
fasteners, and HVAC units faster than inland homes. Rinsing the salt film off
regularly won't make metal immortal, but it slows the whole process down and
stops the rust streaks from staining everything underneath.

## Condos and vacation rentals: curb appeal is income

If you own a rental in Gulf Shores or Orange Beach, the outside of the building
is part of the listing. Guests judge a place the moment they pull up, and a
grimy exterior, green stair rails, or a stained walkway show up in reviews even
when the inside is spotless. Between-season washes — especially before the
spring wave of bookings — keep the photos honest and the first impression
strong. For condo buildings, clean breezeways, railings, and pool decks are the
difference between "well kept" and "tired."

## Why beach homes need washing more often

An inland home in Mobile might do fine with a wash once a year. Beach homes
usually can't stretch that far, because salt keeps landing every day and the
damp film it creates keeps feeding growth. Most coastal homes look noticeably
dirty again faster, so a wash roughly every six months to a year — depending on
how close you are to the water and which way your walls face — keeps the salt
and grime from ever building into a bigger problem.

## Wash it gently

The right way to clean a salt-coated home is [soft washing](/services/house-soft-washing) —
low pressure and a cleaning solution that dissolves the salt film and kills the
mildew, followed by a thorough rinse. High pressure is the wrong tool here.
Beach homes take enough of a beating from the weather; blasting sun-weakened
paint and caulk with a pressure wand just speeds up the damage the salt
started.
